# Mirage architecture review (adversarial), 2026-10-17

Reviewer brief: the design is on trial. Every shortcoming comes with concrete alternatives, a
feasibility sketch on Apple platforms, and an experiment that settles it in a day or two.
Nothing here was run against mac-daddy or the host; every experiment is a proposal.

Sources read: `docs/DESIGN.md` (all), `docs/TOUR-2026-10-17.md`, `experiments/*/README.md`,
`README.md`, the M1/M2 hand-over notes, and the code in `Sources/` (DesktopStreamer,
HEVCEncoder, RateController, LinkTier, TileTracker, ShiftDetector, Predictor, Compositor,
ScreenCapturer, InputInjector, WindowTracker, Wire/Video, Streaming, ViewerSession). Web sources
are linked inline.

Evidence labels used throughout: **M** = measured in this repo (DESIGN §6, experiments),
**E** = estimate derived from those numbers, **V** = vendor claim, **L** = literature or spec,
**C** = read from the code.

## 0. Verdict

The architecture is sound and better-measured than most shipping products. The three core
decisions that matter at 1 Mbps and 200 ms are right and are already paying off: a hybrid
codec with pixel-exact tiles for sparse change, a delay-based rate controller that keeps one
to two frames in flight, and client-side prediction reconciled by host copy-rects with
input-sequence epochs. The M2 result (2 Mbps delivered with a 30 ms queue where M1 saw 567 ms
of sRTT) is the proof.

What is wrong is narrower than "the design" and mostly fixable in days:

1. **Loss handling contradicts the design's own physics.** A single lost 1.2 KB datagram
   costs a 93 KB keyframe and ~2 s of frozen video (M). At 200 ms RTT a fragment
   retransmission costs 1.2 KB and ~230 ms. "Never retransmit" is right for frames and wrong
   for fragments. Parity on keyframes (2.5 % overhead) removes the worst case entirely. (§2.3)
2. **The control stream is an unbudgeted bitrate hole.** Window lists go out as full JSON
   arrays on every change, up to 40 Hz during a drag (C). With 30 windows that is on the order
   of 1.5 Mbps (E), more than the whole link, outside the rate controller, on the same stream
   as input. Needs one measurement and a delta encoding. (§2.2)
3. **Fragments are sent as a burst, not paced.** A 93 KB recovery keyframe is 78 datagrams
   dumped into a 1 Mbps bottleneck at once: 750 ms of queue and the likeliest packet to lose
   (M1 says exactly this). Pace within the frame. (§2.1)
4. **Copy-rects wait behind refinement on one reliable stream** (C): up to ~180 ms of extra
   confirmation latency on a scroll at 800 kbps (E). Split interactive from refinement. (§2.1)
5. **The resolution ladder never engaged on full-screen motion** because it triggers on
   bitrate density, not achieved frame rate; 4 fps at 800 kbps is the result (M). (§2.2)
6. **Bytes are re-sent for pixels the client has already seen**: glyphs, caret blinks,
   re-exposed windows, scrolled-back content. Every mature protocol (RDP, SPICE, NX) has a
   cache; Mirage has none. (§3.1, §3.2)

The speculative directions the brief asked about mostly do not survive scrutiny at 200 ms RTT:
neural frame extrapolation and host-side speculative pre-encoding are rejected with reasons
(§3.6, §3.9). What survives is deterministic and specific: caches, text-run echo for AppKit,
press-feedback from Accessibility roles, and two cheap experiments on Apple's own VideoToolbox
frame processors for the ladder's upscaler.

### 1.2 Apple High Performance screen sharing, specifically

This is the closest competitor on this hardware and the reference point for the Local tier.
Facts (Apple's docs and 9to5Mac's hands-on; no independent latency measurement exists):

- Requires Apple silicon on both Macs and macOS 14+. Recommended **75 Mbps wired for one 4K**
  virtual display; UDP 5900–5902; one session per Mac. Capped at 4K (3840×2160 or 1920×1080
  HiDPI) per virtual display, one or two virtual displays. ([Apple](https://support.apple.com/guide/remote-desktop/apdf8e09f5a9/mac), [9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2026/02/05/how-to-use-macos-high-performance-screen-sharing-for-lower-latency-and-better-color-video/))
- 30 or 60 fps, **4:4:4 chroma**, HDR reference mode (with the P3-ST 2084 display preset),
  stereo audio, Dynamic Resolution. Reviewers call it "almost native"; nobody has published
  milliseconds.
- **The host's physical displays are blanked** while the session runs; the Mac becomes a
  headless renderer for the client.

What this means for Mirage:

- Apple's design point is 75 Mbps; Mirage's is 1 Mbps. Apple's mode is the thing to beat in
  the Local tier (§3.9 of DESIGN), not in Constrained. The honest expectation is that Mirage
  will not beat it on a LAN in M2–M4; it should match it on latency (hardware path is the
  same) and lose on 4:4:4 and HDR until those are built.
- Apple's virtual display is the right model for a client-shaped desktop and for multi-display
  (§2.5). Astropad does the same with a private API (`LiquidVirtualDisplayScreenInfo`, from the
  teardown). Mirage's "capture a physical display" model is the right default for *this* use
  (phrz's monitors are attached) but the virtual-display option should exist.
- 4:4:4 through the hardware HEVC encoder is undocumented on Apple silicon (profile keys that
  appear in `VTSessionCopySupportedPropertyDictionary` but not in headers; developers report
  they work on recent macOS, [Apple forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/837412)).
  Mirage's lossless tiles *are* 4:4:4 for everything that settles, which is the better answer
  at 1 Mbps. Only text *in motion* has the 4:2:0 halo, and for 300 ms.

**Experiment (one afternoon, no host changes):** run Apple's High Performance mode between the
two Macs through `dnctl` pipes at 1 Mbps / 200 ms, and at 20 Mbps / 10 ms. Record whether it
connects, its frame rate, and a clock-photo latency at each. This fixes the two ends of
Mirage's tier table with Apple's own numbers and tells you how far the Local tier has to go.

### 1.3 What Mirage does that none of them do

- A rate controller that treats **one-way queuing delay** as the primary signal and keeps
  1–2 frames in flight, tuned for a tailnet whose dispersion estimate reads the physical path
  rather than the congested hop (M). Parsec's BUD and Sunshine are loss-driven; Citrix and
  PCoIP are proprietary but documented as bandwidth-estimate driven.
- **Input-sequence epochs on every pixel update** (video header, tile batch, copy-rect carry
  the last input they reflect) so a client prediction can be retired by *any* host update, not
  only by a matching copy-rect. This is mosh's epoch idea applied to a pixel stream and I have
  not found it elsewhere.
- **Copy-rect-reconciled local scroll and window drag.** Browsers do local scroll, RDP and VNC
  have copy-rect as an encoding, nobody combines the client-side move with host confirmation
  and confidence back-off.
- **A tile mask inside a full-frame HEVC stream** so a keyframe or a motion frame never
  degrades a tile the client already has pixel-exact. Citrix and RDP composite regions; they
  do not mask a single video stream per tile.
- No accounts, no relay, no keychain: tailnet plus PAKE plus signed transcripts. Every
  competitor that works across the internet carries an identity service.
- The native menu bar / dock plan (§3.8) and the link-tier design with an Extreme mode.

## 2. Performance left on the table

### 2.1 Latency budget, line by line

Click-to-pixel at 800 kbps on the Japan↔Dallas link is RTT + ~40 ms ≈ 250 ms (M). The physics
floor at this RTT is RTT + capture interval/2 + encode + serialization + decode + present. The
table lists what each stage costs today, what the hardware allows, and what to do about it.

| Stage | Today | Floor on this hardware | Gap and fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Input event → client send | Clicks/keys: immediate. Mouse moves coalesced to 15 ms with a 16 ms deferred flush (C: `ViewerSession.sendMouseMove`) | 0–8 ms | Up to 31 ms added to *moves*. Irrelevant for the cursor (local) but it delays drag reconciliation and hover. Coalesce on the display link instead (one move per frame, no timer). ~1 ms gain in feel, free. |
| Input encoding | JSON per event on the control stream (C) | Binary, ~16 B | Negligible latency; matters for Extreme-tier bytes and for parsing cost. Low priority. |
| Control-stream contention | Host→client window list (JSON, whole list, on every change, min 25 ms interval = up to 40 Hz) shares the reliable control stream with cursor position (20 Hz), cursor PNGs, acks (C) | One small stream for input, separate for metadata | See §2.2: possibly **>1 Mbps** during a drag. Input is client→host so it is not queued behind host→client data, but the *link* is. Fix: delta window lists, binary, rate-budgeted. |
| Host apply → app render → compose | CGEvent post to HID tap; app renders within its own cadence; Window Server composites at the next display refresh; host stamps inputs ≥40 ms before capture (C) | ~8–20 ms, not Mirage's | Fine. The 40 ms stamp margin is conservative but harmless. |
| Capture | SCK on change, `minimumFrameInterval` 1/30 (C). Mean wait to next capture ≈ 17 ms + SCK's own delivery latency (one display frame, 8–16 ms) | 60 Hz capture: mean 8 ms | Capture at 60 Hz and let the dirty-area fps cap and the controller decide what to *encode*. Sparse updates (a glyph) would then leave ~8 ms earlier. Cost: tile diff runs twice as often (see next row). |
| Dirty tile diff | CPU, per-pixel with ±4 tolerance inside SCK dirty rects, single thread (C). For a 2560×1440 full-dirty frame ≈ 3.7 Mpx of compares: on the order of 5–15 ms (E); 4× that at 5K | Metal compute: <1 ms (Astropad does exactly this) | The design calls GPU diff "optional". It is optional at 1440p and mandatory for 5K and multi-display (§2.5). Experiment: time the diff at 5K full-dirty in loopback. |
| Encode | 10 ms per frame, 3–16 ms in seqbench (M) | Hardware; this is it | None. `encoding` flag drops captures while busy; at 30 Hz with 10 ms encode that is fine. |
| Pacing | Frame fragments sent back-to-back (C: `for d in frags { out.sendDatagram(d) }`). A 25 KB motion frame = 21 datagrams instantly; a 93 KB recovery keyframe = 78 | Paced at ~1.25× target: datagrams leave at the rate the bottleneck drains | Bursts build a queue at the 1 Mbps hop equal to the frame's serialization time and are where loss happens (M1: "a 107-datagram burst is itself the likeliest thing to lose"). WebRTC and Sunshine both pace. Fix: a sender timer that emits fragments at `videoKbps × 1.25`. Side effect: the dispersion-based capacity estimate must be marked as paced (or use the first N fragments unpaced as the probe train). |
| Serialization at 1 Mbps | 3–8 KB motion frames: 24–64 ms. 25 KB full-screen frames: 200 ms. Keyframes 18–93 KB: 150–750 ms (M) | Bytes are the only lever | This is where the work is. §2.2 and §3. |
| Wire | ~80–110 ms one way (M) | Fixed | None. |
| Reassembly + decode | Decode 2 ms (M). Reassembly completes on the last fragment (counted in serialization) | Same | None. |
| Tile path HOL | Lossless tiles, copy-rects and refinement share **one** reliable QUIC stream (C). A refinement batch may be up to half a second's tile budget (`tileTokens` cap: 0.5 × 45 % × 800 kbps ≈ 22 KB ≈ 180 ms at 1 Mbps) and a copy-rect queued behind it waits | Two streams, interactive first | The scroll prediction hides this on screen, but the newly exposed strip and the confirmation both arrive late, so mismatches surface late and the neutral band lingers. Fix: `interactive-tiles` (copy-rects, input-caused sparse rects) and `refinement` streams; the app pacer never hands refinement bytes to the connection while interactive bytes are pending and keeps ≤ 1 RTT of refinement queued. Network.framework exposes no QUIC stream priority, so the pacer must do it. A lost tile packet also stalls the whole stream for one RTT; with two streams only refinement stalls. |
| Present | Compositor renders on a CADisplayLink tick (C) | Present on decode | Mean half a display interval (4–8 ms) lost waiting for the tick. Minor; fix by requesting a render immediately after `applyVideo`/`applyTiles` when the display link allows. |

Totals: the pipeline is within ~15–25 ms of the hardware floor for a single small update. The
two real latency defects are structural (burst pacing, shared tile stream), not per-stage. After
those, latency gains come only from sending fewer bytes and from prediction.

Two measurement notes:

- `captureNanos` is the SCK callback time, not the sample buffer's presentation timestamp (C).
  The display-time → callback latency inside SCK is therefore invisible in "frame age". Record
  both; it is one line and it makes the budget honest.
- The ARD side-by-side procedure in DESIGN §6 (clock photos) is still the only end-to-end
  method. Keep it and run it against Apple High Performance mode as well (§1.2).

|---|---|---|---|

Matters for the Extreme tier (64 kbps) and for battery on iOS. |

| **Full-screen motion** | 25 KB per HEVC frame at 2560×1440 → **4–5 fps** at 800 kbps and 2 Mbps caps (M) | The ladder stayed at rung 0 because the trigger is `target < 0.18 kbps/kpixel` (= 663 kbps at 1440p) and the target was 800 (C) | Trigger the ladder on **achieved video fps** (e.g. < 10 fps for > 1 s while video is active) and climb back on fps > 25 with headroom. | **Text in motion** | 4:2:0 chroma, 36–38 dB at 3 KB (M) | Chroma halo on coloured text for ≤300 ms | Acceptable. The AVC444 trick (a second stream carrying chroma as luma) would double motion bytes; refinement makes it moot. Reject. |

**The control stream, quantified (needs one measurement).** `WindowTracker.sample` builds a
`WindowInfo` per on-screen window (id, pid, title, frame, z, scroll rect) and
`DesktopStreamer.captured` sends the *whole list* as JSON whenever anything changed (C). During
a drag every sample changes, the sampler runs every 25 ms, so the list goes out at up to 40 Hz.
A `WindowInfo` in JSON is ~150–200 B (E); 30 on-screen windows ≈ 5 KB per message ≈ **1.6 Mbps**
at 40 Hz; 10 windows ≈ 0.5 Mbps. None of this is seen by the rate controller or the pacer,
and it travels on a reliable stream that will retransmit it on loss. If mac-daddy has many
windows, this alone explains any drag that feels worse than loopback. Fix: send only the
windows whose fields changed (binary: id + frame + z ≈ 20 B), cap at 20 Hz, and charge the
bytes to the tile budget. Experiment: add control-stream bytes/s to the 5 s host log line and
run `--scenario drag:30` with 30 windows open. Half a day including the fix.

### 2.3 Loss handling

What happens today on one lost datagram (C + M): the assembler declares the frame lost after
the reorder window or timeout; the viewer acks `lost`; the host tears down the encoder, builds
a new session so the QP floor applies, encodes a keyframe (18–93 KB), and the viewer skips
decoding until it arrives. Measured cost: ~2 s of frozen video at 800 kbps (M), 1 loss per
60 s in good conditions, 10 per 60 s at 2 Mbps in M1.

The design's rule "a lost frame is skipped, never retransmitted" is right about *frames*: a
stale frame is worthless. It is wrong about *fragments*, and the arithmetic at Mirage's own
operating point shows it:

| Recovery | Link time | Freeze | Bytes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Keyframe (today) | 150–750 ms serialization + encoder rebuild | ~2 s (M) | 18–93 KB |
| **NACK one fragment** | 1 RTT + ≤1 frame interval ≈ 230 ms | ≈ 230 ms, and only the frames that depend on it | 1.2 KB |
| XOR/RS parity on the frame | 0 | 0 | +1/k of the frame (12–33 % on 3–8-fragment frames) |

Retransmission beats a keyframe whenever RTT < keyframe bytes / link rate + rebuild cost.
At 1 Mbps that is any RTT under ~700 ms; in the Extreme tier (64 kbps, 1 s RTT, 20 KB
keyframe = 2.5 s) it *still* wins. Sunshine ships 20 % Reed–Solomon by default
([Sunshine config](https://docs.lizardbyte.dev/projects/sunshine/latest/md_docs_2configuration.html));
WebRTC uses NACK first and FEC when loss is high ([Google, ICIP 2013](https://research.google/pubs/handling-packet-loss-in-webrtc/)).

Recommendation, in order:

1. **Fragment NACK.** Host keeps the last ~2 s of sent fragments (≤ 250 KB at 1 Mbps). The
   viewer detects a gap when a later fragment or frame arrives and sends `nack(frameSeq,
   fragIndex)` on the control stream immediately (not after the reorder timeout). The host
   resends if the frame is younger than ~1 RTT + one frame interval, else ignores and lets the
   existing loss path run. The decoder is fed the completed frame late; frames that arrived in
   between are decoded after it (they are P-frames referencing it) or dropped if the viewer is
   more than two frames behind. Expected: freeze 2 s → ~250 ms on isolated losses, which are
   the common case (0–4 per minute, M).
2. **Parity on recovery and key frames always.** Two RS parity shards over a 78-datagram
   keyframe cost 2.5 % and remove the single worst event. A small GF(256) Reed–Solomon in
   Swift is ~200 lines; XOR (k ≤ 8) is 20 lines and enough for motion frames.
3. **Adaptive parity on motion frames** only when measured loss > 0.5 %, as the Extreme tier
   already plans. Below that, NACK is cheaper.
4. **Temporal layering as cheap resilience.** The low-latency encoder exposes
   `BaseLayerBitRateFraction` (DESIGN §2). With two temporal layers, losing an enhancement-layer
   frame breaks nothing the base layer needs; the next base frame decodes. Efficiency cost
   ~10–20 % (L, typical for 2-layer SVC). Experiment: encode seqbench scroll with two layers,
   drop random L1 frames, confirm the VT decoder continues without error.
5. **LTR.** The M1 failure (every refresh frame → `kVTVideoDecoderBadDataErr` after the viewer
   skipped frames) looks like a decoder-side policy bug, as DESIGN already suspects: HEVC
   decoders expect the reference set in order and VideoToolbox is not tolerant of gaps. Test:
   feed every frame after the loss to the decoder (decode-with-errors, display nothing) and
   see whether the refresh frame then decodes. If yes, LTR works and recovery costs a P-frame
   (vendor talks claim 40–50 % smaller than a keyframe for LTR-based recovery, V). One day.

**Experiment that de-risks all of it (one day, loopback):** `dnctl` pipe at 1 Mbps, 200 ms,
1 % loss; run `--headless --scenario scroll:40` for 60 s; report frozen-video seconds and
recovery bytes per loss, before and after NACK + keyframe parity.

### 2.4 Audio (absent)

Apple High Performance mode and Parsec ship it; a remote desktop without audio loses the
"it is my Mac" feeling the moment a notification chimes. Design that fits the budget:

- SCK `capturesAudio` on the same stream (one more output type), 48 kHz stereo.
- Opus at 24–32 kbps for music, 16 kbps for speech, 10 ms frames (Xiph recommends 10 ms as
  the interactive floor; 2.5–5 ms frames exist but cost efficiency, [Opus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opus_(audio_format))).
  Datagrams, one packet per frame, Opus in-band FEC (LBRR) on, which recovers single losses at
  ~1.5× bitrate for the protected band. Budget: 30–50 kbps of the 1 Mbps, off in Extreme.
- Latency: SCK audio delivery ~10–20 ms (unmeasured; the forum notes clock-domain quirks),
  encode 1 ms, jitter buffer 2–3 frames for the 30–45 ms jitter measured on this link, one-way
  100 ms: ~150–170 ms mouth-to-ear. Video frame age is 100–125 ms, so A/V stay within 50 ms
  without explicit sync; stamp both with the host capture clock anyway.
- Encoder availability: AudioToolbox has had the Opus *format* since macOS 10.13 (decode
  confirmed; encode via `AudioConverter` is not confirmed by any source I found). Check at
  runtime with `kAudioConverterEncodeFormats`; if absent, vendor libopus the way libwebp was.
- Experiment: half a day to capture SCK audio → Opus → loopback → play, and measure
  mouth-to-ear with a clap on the host mic recorded on both ends.

### 2.5 Multi-display

mac-daddy has two displays; the design captures one. The pieces exist (one `SCStream` per
display, `displayID` in the stream request), the missing parts are:

- One encoder and one tile tracker per display sharing *one* rate controller and pacer, with a
  priority rule: the display under the pointer or holding the focused window gets the video
  budget; the other gets refinement only (NX's lazy policy, Extreme's spotlight rule).
- Cursor and input coordinates in a global space; the local cursor crossing displays.
- Cost: an idle display costs nothing (SCK on change). The 5K display at 2× is 14.7 Mpx; the
  CPU diff and canvas copies (§2.1) do not scale to it. GPU diff becomes mandatory and the
  ladder floor for the 5K should be 0.5× so a keyframe is ≤ 50 KB.
- Virtual displays (Apple's model, Astropad's too) give a client-shaped desktop for the iPad
  and iPhone (M4) without the aspect-fit letterboxing; the API is private
  (`CGVirtualDisplay`), which is a real risk for a notarized app. Defer; note it.

Milestone: M3 (window metadata already spans displays).

### 2.6 HDR / EDR

Feasible on this hardware: SCK `captureDynamicRange` (macOS 15+) with 10-bit YCbCr, HEVC
Main10 hardware encode, VT decode to 10-bit, `CAMetalLayer.wantsExtendedDynamicRange`. Costs:
~25 % more video bits, and the lossless path breaks (WebP is 8-bit; 10-bit lossless needs
JPEG XL or a custom path). A forum report suggests SCK may still deliver tone-mapped values
in some configurations ([Apple forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/815971)).
Verdict: a Local-tier feature for after M4; irrelevant at 1 Mbps.

### 2.7 HEVC is the ceiling, so send fewer pixels (and sometimes a different codec)

Apple's hardware encoder: HEVC/H.264 4:2:0 8/10-bit, no screen-content tools (palette,
intra block copy), 4:4:4 undocumented. Its cost curve on desktop content is measured:
~3 KB per frame for a 1200×900 scroll at 36–38 dB (M). The levers that remain:

1. **Fewer pixels per frame**: ladder (fix the trigger, §2.2), mask (done), spotlight (only the
   focused window at full rate, Extreme item 3; it belongs in Constrained too when video is
   active in more than one window).
2. **Fewer frames**: prediction lets video fps drop without the user noticing (a confirmed
   copy-rect at 10 fps with local displacement at 120 Hz looks like 120 Hz).
3. **A different codec for small motion regions.** AV1's screen-content tools (palette,
   IBC) are what desktop content wants and the hardware lacks. Software AV1 of a ≤1 Mpx region
   at 10–15 fps is feasible on an M3 Ultra (SVT-AV1 at its fastest presets does 1080p real-time
   on many-core CPUs; Astropad ships rav1e). Chrome Remote Desktop moved to AV1 I444 for screen
   content ([WebRTC commit](https://webrtc.googlesource.com/src/+/59020bd88b968df71e3789a4f00355b76cc8b929%5E%21/)).
   Decode on the client is dav1d (software, fine at these sizes; iOS has hardware AV1 decode on
   A17 Pro/M3+). Experiment (one day, after vendoring SVT-AV1 or rav1e): seqbench scroll region
   at 600 kbps, HEVC hardware vs AV1 screen-content preset; compare bytes at equal PSNR and
   encode ms. Verdict pending the number; the vendoring cost is real, so this is M5+.
4. **No** to AVC444-style chroma streams (double the motion bytes) and **no** to 4:4:4
   hardware profiles until Apple documents them.

## 3. Novel directions, judged on merit

Format per item: mechanism → expected gain → risk → validating experiment → verdict.

### 3.1 Content-addressed rect cache (pursue, M2.5)

Mechanism: the host hashes the pixels of every lossless rect it sends (128-bit, e.g. two
xxhash64 lanes) and keeps an LRU of hashes it believes the client holds (bounded, e.g.
64 MB of pixels ≈ 40 000 text-tile-sized rects). If a rect to send hashes to a known entry,
send `cached(rect, id)` (≈ 16 B) instead of the WebP. The client keeps the same LRU keyed by
id and blits from it. The hash is of the *source* pixels, so this is RDP's bitmap cache and
SPICE's GLZ dictionary at rect granularity, without hooking any drawing API.

What it covers: every glyph in a monospace terminal or code editor after its first appearance;
both caret states; menu re-opens; tab switches; window re-exposure after a drag or a Space
switch; dialog re-appearance; dock hover animations; anything periodic.

Expected gain (E): typing in Terminal/Xcode 30 kbps → < 5 kbps; a window re-exposed after
being covered costs ~1 KB instead of its full lossless size (tilebench: 220–300 KB for a
window; 1.5–2 s at 1 Mbps). Caret blink 4 kbps → 0.3 kbps.

Risk: hash collisions (use 128 bits; negligible), cache coherence across reconfigure/reset
(clear on `reset`), host memory for the pixel copies (keep only hashes on the host, pixels on
the client), the tight-box optimisation making rects slightly different each time (a glyph's
box differs by anti-aliasing with its neighbours; mitigate by snapping cache candidates to
8-px-aligned boxes).

Experiment (one day): instrument `submitLossless` to hash every rect and count hits against
a session-long set; run `--scenario typing:30` and 5 minutes of real use on loopback; report
hit rate and bytes that would have been saved. If hit rate < 40 % on typing, reject.

### 3.2 Scrollback canvas (pursue, M5)

Mechanism: per window, the client keeps pixels that a confirmed copy-rect scrolled *out* of
the scroll rect in an offscreen extension of the canvas (a per-window texture with a growing
y-range, bounded to e.g. 8 screens). When a later scroll moves content back into view, the
client fills the exposed band from the scrollback instead of the neutral colour, and the host,
which can track the same history from its own copy-rects, sends `copyFromHistory` (a copy-rect
whose source is a history offset) instead of lossless strips.

Expected gain: scrolling back over content you just read costs ~0 bytes and shows real
content at 0 ms instead of a grey band for RTT + strip time. Reading code and documents is
mostly this motion.

Risk: content that changed while off-screen (live logs, chat) shows stale pixels until the
host's update arrives; acceptable because the host's lossless refinement still runs on
mismatch (the design already has the mechanism). Memory: 8 screens × 1440p × 4 B = 118 MB per
window; cap per window and total.

Experiment (one day): testbench `scroll:40` down then up; measure bytes and `crispMs` in the
second half with and without the history. Expected: second half → near zero bytes.

### 3.3 Text-run echo for AppKit apps (pursue as M5 caret echo; reject as "text mode" for the picture)

The brief asks whether identical fonts and UI frameworks on both ends can be exploited by
re-rendering text locally from recognised runs.

Mechanism that works: Accessibility on the host gives, for AppKit/SwiftUI text (probes:
Mail, Finder, Sketch, Xcode, Terminal, Notes), the focused element, caret bounds
(`AXBoundsForRange` at the insertion point), and the font (`AXFont` dict). The client renders
the typed glyph with Core Text in that font at that position, underlined mosh-style, and
clears it when the host's pixels (which carry the input seq) cover it. This is DESIGN §3.5
item 4 and it is right; mosh's numbers (70 % of keystrokes predicted, 0.9 % wrong, corrected
within one RTT) are the realistic target for the AppKit share of apps.

Why "text mode" (the text runs *are* the picture, Extreme item 5) should not be built as a
rendering path: both ends have the *system* fonts, not user-installed ones; macOS text
rendering is not pixel-identical across scale factors, font-smoothing settings, subpixel
positioning (fractional advances), and background colour blending; and AX attributed strings
do not carry layout (line breaks, ligatures, kerning adjustments by the app). A locally
rendered paragraph would differ from the host by a few pixels everywhere and the lossless
refinement would then re-send it all, costing *more*. Use text runs as a **prediction overlay**
only, where a few pixels of error are corrected by the confirming tiles, never as the final
picture. At 64 kbps the Extreme tier should instead rely on the rect cache (§3.1) plus
paletted tiles.

Risk: AX query cost per keystroke (a few ms of IPC, measured for the scroll-area query; the
attributed-string query for a whole document is expensive, so query only the caret line);
Chromium/Electron excluded (probe); terminals vary.

Experiment (one day): in Xcode, Terminal and Mail, on each keystroke query caret bounds and
font, render the glyph locally, and compare against the captured pixels one capture later:
histogram of position error in px and of glyph-width error. If p95 error ≤ 2 px, build it.

### 3.4 Glyph-atlas / font-aware pixel coding (reject; subsumed by 3.1)

Mechanism: segment glyph bitmaps in text tiles (connected components), match against an atlas
both ends can render from font name + size, send atlas ids and positions. RDP did this in the
GDI era with a glyph cache fed by the drawing API; without that hook, the host must recognise
glyphs from pixels.

Why it fails here: macOS glyphs come in subpixel-phase variants (fractional advances → 4–8
bitmaps per glyph), font-smoothing variants, and anti-aliased edges blended with the
background, so the atlas key is (glyph, phase, fg, bg). Recognition errors produce wrong
characters, the worst artifact a remote desktop can have. And the content-addressed rect cache
(§3.1) captures the same repetition at rect granularity with zero recognition risk and no
font knowledge.

Experiment that would change my mind (half a day, offline): over a typing session's rects,
cluster by exact pixels; if ≥ 90 % of glyph *occurrences* are exact repeats of an earlier
rect, 3.1 gets them; if they are repeats only up to subpixel phase (i.e. 3.1 misses them), a
phase-tolerant cache key (quantise the box origin to the pixel grid before hashing) is the
fix, still without recognition.

### 3.5 UI-element-aware coding and press/hover feedback (pursue the feedback, reject the rest)

Sending the AX tree and rendering natively generalises §3.8 (menu bar, dock) into rebuilding
AppKit on the client. Reject beyond chrome: custom views, web content and Electron make the
fallback path the common path, and two renderings of one window fight each other.

The cheap, valuable piece is **press feedback from roles**, which DESIGN §3.5 item 5 skips as
"not generalizable" and §3.9 item 1 then reintroduces for Extreme. Be consistent: it is
generalizable for the standard roles and it belongs in every tier.

Mechanism: the host already tracks the cursor position at 20 Hz. Add an AX hit-test
(`AXUIElementCopyElementAtPosition`, a few ms, off the capture queue) when the pointer rests
for > 50 ms, and ship (role, frame, enabled) of the element under the pointer with the cursor
position. On mouse-down the client tints the element's rect with the system pressed overlay
(a translucent darkening, not the real artwork) for roles button, checkbox, radio, pop-up,
segmented control, tab, list/outline row, menu item; on mouse-up it removes it, and the host's
pixels (input-stamped) take over. Hover: same for rows and tabs that highlight on hover.

Expected gain: click feedback at 0 ms instead of ~250 ms for standard controls in AppKit apps,
which is most of Finder, Mail, Xcode, System Settings. Risk: wrong element under a fast-moving
pointer (only tint when the hit-test is fresher than the last move); custom controls get
nothing (fine).

Experiment (half a day): measure hit-test latency per rest event on mac-daddy's apps; count
how many clicks in a 10-minute session land on a role in the list.

### 3.6 Frame extrapolation and input-conditioned pixel prediction (reject for pixels in general; one narrow experiment)

What exists: NVIDIA Reflex 2 warps the last rendered frame to the newest camera pose and
inpaints holes from depth and history (V); Outatime masks up to 120 ms with game-state
speculation plus image-based rendering and rollback at a large bandwidth cost (L);
Stadia's "negative latency" was speculative frame branches, never demonstrated publicly.
All three need a model of what the input *does*: a camera, a physics state.

Desktop UI has no depth and no continuous camera. Its input-driven motion is piecewise and
already enumerated: scroll and drag (exact, deterministic, handled by copy-rect prediction),
caret motion (handled by 3.3), press states (3.5), window resize (M3, same as drag with a
stretch). Everything else that moves (video playback, animations, progress bars) is not
input-driven and gains nothing from input conditioning. A learned extrapolator adds 5–20 ms
of inference and produces artifacts exactly where the user looks. At 200 ms RTT it would have
to invent 6 frames of UI. Reject.

The one narrow place a frame processor helps is **non-interactive video regions at 4–5 fps**
(the lock-screen case, video playback): interpolating between two received frames adds one
frame interval of latency (200–250 ms at 4 fps), which is unacceptable for anything the user
drives but fine for a movie. Apple ships this: VideoToolbox's `VTFrameProcessor` (macOS
15.4+) offers low-latency frame interpolation and low-latency super-resolution designed for
real-time use ([WWDC25 session 300](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/300)).
Experiment (one day): classify a video region as "playback" when it is dirty every capture for
> 2 s with no input in its window; feed decoded frames through low-latency frame interpolation
on the client; measure added latency and GPU/ANE ms at 1440p. Verdict after the number.

### 3.7 Super-resolution for the ladder (pursue a one-day comparison)

Today MetalFX spatial upscales lower rungs (C). MetalFX is tuned for anti-aliased 3-D game
frames and rings on text. Options:

- `VTLowLatencySuperResolutionScaler` (the VTFrameProcessor family above), built for video
  calls; runs on ANE/GPU; latency and text behaviour unknown until measured.
- A tiny text-aware CNN (Core ML, 4–8 layers, trained on synthetic renders of macOS text at
  0.5× → 1×). ANE dispatch overhead is ~0.1 ms per call on M4 ([ANE benchmarks](https://maderix.substack.com/p/inside-the-m4-apple-neural-engine-615)); a 1440p pass through a
  small network is plausibly 3–8 ms (E). Training data is free (render text with Core Text).
  Glyph-aware SR is an active research area ([GlyphSR, AAAI 2025](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/32893)) but those models are far too large for a frame loop.
- Do nothing: refinement delivers crisp text 300 ms after motion stops; the upscaler only
  matters during motion, where the eye is tolerant.

Experiment (one day): seqbench scroll at 0.5× → upscale with MetalFX spatial, VT low-latency
SR, bilinear; report PSNR against the native capture and a 10-word legibility read by a human.
If VT SR is within 2 ms and visibly better on text, switch; otherwise keep MetalFX and stop.

### 3.8 FEC vs retransmission at this RTT

Covered in §2.3: NACK first (wins at every RTT Mirage targets), parity on keyframes always,
adaptive parity on motion frames above 0.5 % loss, temporal layers as the structural
resilience the hardware gives for free. RaptorQ (RFC 6681/6330) is overkill for 3–80
fragment blocks; XOR for k ≤ 8 and a small Reed–Solomon for keyframes are enough.

### 3.9 Speculative pre-encoding of likely next frames on the host (reject, with one surviving idea)

Variants the brief lists, each against the physics:

- **The frame after a scroll step, sent before the input arrives.** The host cannot capture
  pixels that are not on its screen, and it must not scroll the app speculatively (side
  effects, and the user may scroll the other way). For the *backward* direction the client's
  scrollback (3.2) already has the pixels. For forward, AX can supply the *text* of the next
  lines in AppKit text views (a prediction overlay, 3.3), never pixels. Reject.
- **The frame after a keystroke.** Which key? Rendering 50 branches per keystroke is Stadia's
  idea and costs 50× the bytes; mosh-style local echo (3.3) is the correct form of this
  speculation. Reject.
- **Hover/press futures.** Pre-pressing controls has side effects. Reject; 3.5 draws a
  generic pressed state locally instead.
- **Pre-encoded recovery keyframe** kept warm in a second encoder session. Encode is 10 ms;
  the cost is serialization, not encoding. Reject.

What survives: **speculative refinement ordering.** The refinement queue is oldest-first then
centre-first (C). Replace "centre" with a predicted gaze: the caret line, the element under
the pointer, then the focused window, then the rest. Zero bytes, tiles the user is about to
read arrive first. Half a day; measure `crispMs` for the focused window in `scroll:40`.

### 3.10 Neural Engine, anywhere it measurably helps

- Tile classification (text / UI / photo): the colour-count heuristic (M: near-lossless only
  above 4096 colours) is already right; a CNN would not change the bytes. Reject.
- Caret detection for Chromium/Electron (DESIGN §3.5 item 4 fallback: temporal diff + Vision
  text rectangles): sound as designed; `VNDetectTextRectanglesRequest` cost is unpublished,
  measure it. Keep.
- Learned lossless or near-lossless tile codecs: WebP lossless is ~0.15 B/px on text (M);
  learned image codecs gain 10–20 % at orders of magnitude more compute and per-call ANE
  overhead per tile. Reject.
- Super-resolution (3.7) and playback-region interpolation (3.6): the two experiments above.
- Scroll-rect discovery without AX: the line-profile correlation already learns it (M). Reject.

### 3.11 Audio with low delay

§2.4. Build it in M5 with Opus 10 ms frames and in-band FEC; measure SCK audio latency first.

### 3.12 Other things worth a line

- **Deadline-tagged refinement (NX's lazy encoding, made explicit).** Give each refinement rect
  a deadline; drop and re-queue rather than let debt accumulate. The `tileTokens` debt mechanism
  approximates this; an explicit deadline makes the Extreme tier's behaviour predictable.
- **Clock honesty.** Use SCK's presentation timestamp for `captureNanos` (§2.1).
- **Decode-with-errors policy** in the viewer (feed every frame, display only valid ones) is
  needed both for LTR (§2.3) and for NACK'd late frames.

### 4.1 Things the current design gets right and must not be broken

- **Delay-based rate control with 1–2 frames in flight.** The 2 Mbps result (queue ≤30 ms,
  p95 frame age 125 ms, where M1 saw 567 ms sRTT) is the single most important measurement in
  the repo. Any pacing or FEC change must keep the in-flight cap and the ×0.85-per-RTT back-off.
- **Pixel-exact tiles for sparse change, never video for a typed glyph, tight boxes.**
  Measured and correct (1 KB at 20 dB vs 0.55 KB lossless).
- **The tile mask inside full-frame HEVC and the client canvas model.** Lossless tiles survive
  keyframes; ordering is free on one Metal queue. Do not go back to per-region encoders.
- **Input-sequence stamping of every pixel update** and the predictor's epoch/confidence
  rules. The whole latency-hiding story depends on it.
- **The tolerant diff (±4) and tolerant shift verification.** Exact matching never fired on
  real captures (M); do not "fix" the tolerance away.
- **QP-floored first keyframe from a fresh encoder session.** 72–160 KB → 18 KB.
- **Local cursor with the cursor hidden in capture**, cursor shapes on the control stream.
- **Unchanged frames never submitted to the encoder** (seqbench conclusion 1).
- **Tailnet-only transport, PAKE pairing, transcript-signed sessions, two TCC grants, no
  accounts.** Nothing in this review needs a relay, a keychain or an identity service.
- **Measurement culture.** `experiments/` with READMEs and the headless `--stats` mode are
  why this review could be concrete. Every item above names its experiment for that reason.

### 4.2 Points where I disagree with DESIGN.md as written

- §3.1 "a lost frame is … never retransmitted": right for frames, wrong for fragments (§2.3).
- §3.3 / seqbench conclusion 5 "the ladder's job is the keyframe … not the P-frames": the M2
  full-screen result (25 KB P-frames, 4 fps) shows the ladder's job includes full-screen
  motion; the trigger just never fired (§2.2).
- §3.5 item 5 "hover/press feedback: not generalizable; skipped on purpose" versus §3.9
  item 1 which reintroduces it for Extreme: it is generalizable for standard AX roles and
  should be tier-independent (§3.5).
- §3.9 item 5 "text mode" as the picture at 64 kbps: use text runs as prediction, not as the
  rendered image; use the rect cache and paletted tiles for bytes (§3.3).
- §2 / §3.3 "GPU diff optional": true at 1440p, false at 5K and for multi-display (§2.1, §2.5).
- §0's physics table omits the control stream and treats bytes as video + tiles only. Add a
  third class, "metadata", and budget it (§2.2).
